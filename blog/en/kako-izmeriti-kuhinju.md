Measuring the kitchen is the first hour of the job and the only one you can't repeat from the workshop. Everything you don't write down in the flat you guess later; everything you guess, you fix on installation with a jigsaw. This guide is a list of what to measure, how to measure it and how to enter it in the program afterwards, so the kitchen is designed on the real wall and not the one you remembered.

## Walls: length at three heights

A wall is almost never straight, and two neighbouring measurements differ exactly where you need the millimetre. So every wall length is measured **at three heights**: at the floor, at worktop height (about 850–920 mm from the floor) and at wall-unit height (about 1400–2100 mm). You write down all three and work with the smallest; the difference tells you how much [end panel](../recnik/ukrasna-strana.html) or filler you need to cover the gap.

Measure from corner to corner, from corner to window, from corner to door, and overall. The sum of the parts has to agree with the overall; if it doesn't, measure again, straight away, while you are there.

## Ceiling height and what hangs off the wall

- **Ceiling height** in at least three places along the wall where the kitchen goes; old ceilings drop by several centimetres. It matters for tall units (2000–2200 mm) and for whether the wall run can go at 1400 mm from the floor with a height of 720.
- **Window**: distance from the corner to the edge of the frame, width, sill height from the floor, frame height, width of the sill board if there is one and how far it sticks out. A window above the [sink](../recnik/sudopera-element.html) also decides where the casement may open.
- **Door**: distance from the corner, width with the frame, opening direction. The leaf must not hit the fronts.
- **Radiator**: distance from the corner, width, height from the floor, depth with the valve. Pipes running along the wall too.
- **Skirting, trims, pipes along the ceiling**: anything that stops the carcass sitting against the wall, with how far it sticks out.

## Services: by height and from the corner

Every connection is written down with **two numbers**: how high it is from the floor and how far it is from the nearest corner. The third piece of data is the wall it is on. Without those three the connection sits “somewhere there” in the design.

- **Water** (hot and cold) and **waste**: height and distance from the corner, diameter of the waste pipe. The waste decides where the sink goes, and the sink unit has no back rail so the trap fits.
- **Power**: every socket and outlet, especially the outlets for the oven, the hob and the hood; height and distance.
- **Gas**: the position of the tap and pipe, because it doesn't move, and the [hood](../recnik/aspirator.html) above gas goes 750 mm above the hob (electric 650), to the manufacturer's instructions.
- **Ventilation**: the opening for the hood duct, height and distance; whether the duct runs through the wall unit or above it.

## Appliances with their sizes

Built-in appliances are not “standard” until you have read the dimension sheet. For each one write down the maker, the model and the installation sizes from the instructions, not the outside sizes of the appliance:

- **Oven**: recess width (the program asks for min. 568 mm), recess height about 590–600, depth 550.
- **Hob**: cut-out sizes to the manufacturer's template, and where its box underneath may go.
- **Dishwasher and washing machine**: width, height with the feet, depth with the hoses; the [plinth](../recnik/cokla.html) goes in front of the dishwasher, so it needs the height.
- **Fridge**: built-in or free-standing, height with the hinges, opening, ventilation clearance to the instructions.
- **Hood**: width 600 or 900, body height and duct.

If the customer hasn't bought the appliances yet, write that down as an open question and design the recesses to sizes you don't deviate from until the sheet arrives.

## Right angle and flatness of the wall

You check a 90° corner without a square: from the corner measure 600 mm along one wall and 800 mm along the other, then measure the distance between those two points. If it is 1000 mm, the corner is square; if it is more, the corner is open, if less, closed. You check the same with the diagonals of the whole room when the kitchen goes on two or three walls.

You check the flatness of the wall with a long straight batten or a level held against the wall, horizontally and vertically, and write down where the belly is and how big. Check the floor with a level along the wall: where the highest point is, that is where the [legs](../recnik/nogica.html) are at their lowest, and everything rises from there (the legs are adjustable, 80–150 mm, default 100).

## Checklist to take with you

- tape measure, laser for distances and diagonals, level, long straight batten
- paper with the floor plan drawn and each wall separately, a coloured pencil for services
- phone: a photo of every wall, every connection and every appliance with its rating plate
- installation instructions for the appliances the customer already has
- questions for the customer: the height of the person who cooks most, left- or right-handed, what bothers them in the old kitchen

## How you enter it in the program

In TOPICA you first create the **room**: the length and width of the room and the ceiling height. Then you add **services** to the wall: the type (water, waste, power, gas, hood, radiator, window), the wall it is on and the position; the marker is dragged with the mouse along the wall until it sits at the measured distance from the corner. Only then do you line up the carcasses against the wall, and the program keeps them in a run, without overlapping, while you see whether the sink is over the waste and whether the oven is far enough from the wall.

A well-measured kitchen is boring on installation, and that is the best compliment measuring can get.
